Flow steering

Steering a model away from
its dangerous attractor

Wiktor Tomasik

Uses a flow model of the conversation to notice when a trajectory is being dragged toward a dangerous basin, then applies the smallest proactive nudge that changes where it ends up.

The idea

Steering out of a dangerous basin

From the trajectories of many safety conversations in a semantic space, we compute a generalized flow: a model of where a conversation tends to drift, and how likely a given drift is to drain into a dangerous basin. The flow is probabilistic: a turn may point elsewhere while paths from its current state still tend to end up in that basin. The method uses that flow to notice when a live trajectory's chance of ending in a dangerous basin is rising, then applies the smallest proactive nudge to the model's internals — steering — that changes where it ends up. Because the nudge compounds over a whole trajectory, it is especially suited to long-running agents — coding sessions, extended reasoning, and other long agent runs — where small early drift accumulates into a bad ending.

The target cases include persistent adversarial goals — one person or a coordinated group gradually probing for a failure — and long-horizon agents whose individually reasonable steps accumulate into unsafe drift over hours of interaction.

These cases require separate evaluation. Deliberate adversarial pressure and endogenous agent drift create different transition distributions, even when both eventually reach a similar unsafe outcome.

When a live conversation is drifting toward a dangerous basin, add the smallest possible nudge to the model's internal state. The nudge should barely move the next-word distribution, yet applied early and repeatedly it may tip the trajectory across the boundary. The model's own flow carries the rest of the turn.

The 3D view shows trajectory drift and later divergence; the correction occurs inside the language model's hidden state. The experiments below measure predictive information in the trace, the local policy change produced by repeated pulses, and the causal evidence still required to connect a particular pulse with a safer future.

TraceScope

How many paths become a flow

The flow comes from TraceScope, a tool I built to map the flow of meaning through a collection of texts. It maps where texts sit and learns how meaning tends to move between them. The pipeline that turns a pile of transcripts into the field above runs in a fixed sequence:

  1. Embed every turn into a high-dimensional vector (here, 3072-d embeddings).
  2. Cluster the turns, auto-selecting the number of clusters by silhouette score.
  3. Reduce to a low-dimensional space with a UMAP/t-SNE search under a cosine metric, keeping the projection that best preserves neighborhoods.
  4. Compute axes with PCA on the reduced coordinates, then have an LLM read each axis's keyword evolution and give it a short, human label.
  5. Label clusters the same way, so each basin gets a name like actionable harm instructions.
  6. Train a flow model — radial basis functions (RBF, used here) or a mixture-density network — that learns a velocity field from the directed turn-to-turn segments of every path.
  7. Bake a velocity grid so any point in the space can be sampled by fast trilinear interpolation. That grid is exactly what the browser advects particles through.

Why the flow matters. A scatter of points tells you where conversations have been. A flow field tells you where a conversation is heading. Danger often lives in the transition: the moment a context starts sliding toward a basin where things go wrong. In the held-out experiment below, the learned high-dimensional flow predicts future conversation states substantially better than keeping the current state fixed.

What an attractor means. Where the field converges, with low speed and inward flow, sits an attractor: a stable endpoint that, once a trajectory enters its basin, the dynamics keep pulling it toward. The attractor inherits an average outcome score from the data around it, so a basin can be read as safer or more dangerous. For control, that interpretation is treated as a candidate until held-out branches confirm both convergence and later outcomes. The clearest illustration is the math-reasoning run below.

The projection is the readout. The low-dimensional view is for human eyes. The final experiment fits the dynamics in a train-only 48-dimensional representation of Qwen's native hidden states, while the intervention lives in the larger activation space. The projection lets us see the basin a conversation is approaching and display any measured counterfactual branch.

The data

The safety dataset behind the flow

The flow is learned from Anthropic's hh-rlhf red-team-attempts subset — 38,961 human/assistant red-team transcripts released with Anthropic's red-teaming work. The reason this dataset fits is structural: every row is a full multi-turn transcript, giving the flow real trajectories. It also ships transcript-level safety signals (min_harmlessness_score_transcript, red-team rating, tags) that become the scalar field colored through the flow.

The prepared visualization keeps 240 stratified paths — 120 dangerous and 120 safe — across roughly 1,500 turns. Safety is a robust-normalized harmlessness score; the dangerous flag is deliberately conservative. Single-turn moderation sets have cleaner per-response labels, but most rows are only one prompt and one response — two-point paths, with no trajectory to steer — which is why the multi-turn red-team transcripts were chosen here.

On this run, TraceScope named the three emergent axes assistant prevalence, informativeness level, and violation domain, and the turns fell into regions like refusing harmful requests, dangerous predatory content, and sexual boundary violations. These labels name the visible map for diagnosis; the control vectors are learned separately from hidden states.

For the new controlled test, Qwen2.5-1.5B generated 400 reactive paths from 50 independent red-team goals: 33 train, 6 validation, and 11 sealed test. The resulting 4,304 assistant responses span a median of 10.5 decisions per path. Qwen3Guard judged every complete-prefix response; 213 were Unsafe and 104 paths contained at least one Unsafe response.

The next user message preserves the original human escalation, but its bridge depends on whether the preceding Qwen answer refused, offered an alternative, asked a question, or continued. An intervention can therefore alter later input through the conversation loop. At every turn, the eight matched paths receive a rotated design of zero, positive and negative safety-direction pulses, orthogonal pulses, and mixed directions, all nonzero actions having the same norm.

Mechanism

Two maps and one lever

Two different spaces are involved, and the method keeps them apart. The first is the TraceScope semantic flow view at the top of this page: a map of where conversations go and which way they tend to move. It supplies a diagnostic projection of long-horizon tendency. The second is the language model's activation space, the hidden state inside the model while it writes. The correction is applied there, and controller validation stays in the native high-dimensional state.

Deciding when to act. A monitor estimates a finite-horizon committor: the probability that the run reaches an unsafe outcome within the next H steps. Its state can include the conversation, recent velocity, memory, tool calls, current plan, and environment observations. Interventions focus on interior states where safe and unsafe futures both remain plausible, before the current response itself requires blocking.

qH(st) = P(unsafe within H steps | trace, memory, tools, plan, observations)
estimate future risk in native state space · display a projection in TraceScope

The central test compares this estimate with a direct future-risk classifier given the same current state. Recent velocity, phase, and history earn a role only when they improve calibration or warning time on held-out trajectories.

Choosing the push. The direction is measured from the model. For each topic, an unsafe continuation and a same-topic safe continuation are run through Qwen, and the difference in their hidden states supplies a candidate safety direction. Because that contrast may also carry topic or change-of-subject information, the controlled experiment adds its opposite, orthogonal equal-norm directions, mixed directions, and zero. This turns the steering direction from an assumption into something that can be measured at matched states.

dsafe = mean( hsafe·same·topic − hunsafe )
ut ∈ {0, +dsafe, −dsafe, dorthogonal}
h17, token ← h17, token + αut
measure a candidate direction · compare it with matched controls · add a small amount during generation

Natural dynamics and control response are separate quantities. The flow estimates how the state changes under ordinary generation. Repeated matched branches estimate the local action effect, represented by the gradient of future risk with respect to the pulse. A useful action needs a stable sign across branches from the same state.

Ju(st) = ∂qH(st; u) / ∂u at u = 0
measure how a small action changes future risk at this state

Applying it. While the monitor is on, a small amount of the selected direction is added to one residual stream at the final prompt token and every generated token. Each addition should barely change the next word; repeated over a turn, the changes may alter which later branch is reached. In the controlled test, 3,766 nonzero interventions produced median next-token KL of 0.000147 and a 95th percentile of 0.001235, both inside the preregistered limits.

A lighter form of the push. Once a local causal response can be estimated reliably, the correction could be packaged as a small receptor, head gate, or narrow adapter that stays quiet outside the uncertain region. The base model remains unchanged. The flow supplies long-horizon context, while the local response model supplies the steering direction.

The two paths in the two trajectories visualize that intended combination: read where the conversation is heading, add a small push in activation space, and project the resulting branch back into the flow map.

Result

Measurements along the path

The animation asks whether a small early correction can change a much later destination. The new experiment measures the two ingredients behind that picture separately: whether a generalized flow predicts where a held-out conversation moves, and whether repeated hidden-state nudges can preserve the model's local policy.

The four figures below come from three analyses. The source line above each title marks the final reactive experiment, the earlier fitted-field diagnostic, or the earlier activation-direction study.

At the primary three-turn horizon, the learned flow's mean endpoint error was 13.51 on validation and 13.58 on the sealed test, compared with 16.33 and 16.03 for persistence. The improvement appears in both held-out splits and across horizons one, three, and five. This establishes predictive structure relative to a fixed-state baseline. The stricter comparison uses a direct future-risk classifier on the same state and measures any additional warning time supplied by the trajectory.

The reduction relative to persistence indicates that recent trace information constrains likely next states. TraceScope expresses this tendency visually by releasing starting points into the fitted field and recording which basin they reach. Basin membership remains a diagnostic summary; the finite-horizon future-risk probability is the quantity needed by a controller.

Across 3,766 nonzero-control turns, median next-token KL was 0.000147 against a limit of 0.001, and the 95th percentile was 0.001235 against a limit of 0.005. These values measure local policy change during generation, before any later projection into the flow view.

Across Qwen models from 0.5B to 14B parameters, the candidate safety direction's measured separation from topic ranges from 64% to 79%. This supplies an activation-space basis for randomized branching; matched local responses determine which direction helps at a particular state.

The next study focuses on state-local action response. Natural dynamics vary strongly across goals, while the effect of a tiny action depends on its application state. The generalized flow estimates the distribution of future states; repeated counterfactual branches estimate how candidate actions change that distribution. TraceScope then displays measured branches, probabilities, and uncertainty.

Next experiment

The path forward

The next implementation tests whether trajectory tendency provides earlier useful information and whether a low-KL action changes the corresponding future-risk probability. Repeated counterfactual branches begin from the same naturally occurring agent states, allowing matched branches to identify a local causal response in native activation space.

  1. Build the full trace state from conversation history, memory, tool calls, plan, environment observations, and native model activations.
  2. Separate the threat models into held-out persistent-adversary and endogenous-drift sets, since each produces a different transition distribution.
  3. Measure lead time against a direct classifier given the same current state, then ablate recent velocity, phase, and history to locate the added predictive information.
  4. Find sensitive interior states where safe and unsafe futures both occur and the estimated future-risk probability can still move.
  5. Branch repeatedly from each state with zero, antithetic, orthogonal, and mixed low-KL actions; estimate the local action gradient and its uncertainty.
  6. Gate the intervention on agreement across matched branches, then measure unsafe outcomes, task success, refusal rate, response quality, and cumulative KL.
  7. Project the measured result into TraceScope with branch probability and uncertainty, keeping the 3D field as a view of high-dimensional evidence.

A valid controller requires earlier calibrated risk than the current-state baseline, a stable local action effect across repeated branches, and retained task performance. Held-out adversarial and drift trajectories provide the test before adaptive intervention begins.
