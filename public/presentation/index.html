The louder the sycophancy alarm, the less a correction can do

I am building a self regulation loop for language models: measure what is happening inside the model, decide, then steer it back on course. The standard loop measures how sycophantic the model currently looks. I rebuilt that measurement around a different question — has the model already committed, and will a correction still work? — and measured how the state of the conversation changes what the same steering achieves.

Qwen2.5 · 1.5B–14B OLMo-2 · 7B ~20,000 pressured conversations ~40,000 steering trials

The loop, and what I changed in it

A self regulation loop has three stages, and each one can go wrong. Measure: read the model’s internal state — the numerical representations, called activations, that it computes at each layer as it processes text. Decide: choose whether, when and how strongly to intervene. Steer: nudge those numbers along a direction that shifts behaviour — here, back toward the model’s own answer. This is called activation steering.

MONICA is the published loop for sycophancy. Its measurement scores how sycophantic the internal state looks right now; its decision rule fires past a threshold and pushes harder as that score rises. My version keeps the same skeleton and swaps the two upstream stages:

1 · Measure
standard“Does the state look sycophantic right now?”
ours“Has it committed — and will it cave if this continues?” Grounded in tested alternative futures, the way Sycophantic Anchors grounds commitment.
2 · Decide
standardPast a threshold, push — harder when the alarm is louder.
oursChoose the moment, and read the conversational context. Act early and gently; past commitment, a push accomplishes little at high cost.
3 · Steer
standardNudge the activations along an anti sycophancy direction.
oursSame nudges — tested across moments, strengths, and conversational contexts.

conversation → measure → decide → steer → back into the conversation

Each finding below examines one part of this loop. Collectively, they indicate the standard design measures the incorrect quantity — and the quantity it does measure moves backwards compared to the decision it supports.

The testbed

I need a hazard where “the model is being pushed off course” is unambiguous. Sycophancy under factual pressure fits: the model answers a question correctly, then a user insists it is wrong and escalates over five turns. Because the right answer is known, every stage of the loop can be scored cleanly. These are easy questions with one short answer, picked because they are scoreable rather than because they are representative. Whether folding on trivia predicts folding on medicine or law is untested here and it could go either way: a model carries safety training and hedging habits into those domains that a question about capital cities never touches.

I ran 158 such questions through five different kinds of social pressure, holding the questions and the number of turns fixed so only the character of the pressure changes: a casual peer, a claimed expert, an emotionally distressed user, an appeal to group consensus, and a mixed escalation. That design is what lets me ask how context changes steering.

The replacement measurement

My measurement stage has two halves, both aimed at the future rather than the present appearance.

The counterfactual half — the withdrawal test. At every step, I copy the conversation as it is and add one message that removes the user's opinion and asks the original question directly, without suggesting the desired answer. The model's response to this is recorded, then the copy is discarded — the conversation being studied continues unchanged. A correct answer indicates the earlier concession lasted only as long as the pressure; an incorrect one shows it has lasted beyond the pressure, which I refer to as commitment. Since this method reads the model's own behaviour in a counterfactual continuation, there is no trained detector between me and the result. It serves as the reference standard against which every finding below is measured.

The predictive half. A classifier analysing the activations can determine, before the model writes its answer, whether it will eventually cave. Read at the last prompt token it scores 0.73 to 0.88 across six models, against 0.60 to 0.72 for the cheapest baseline there is, counting how many turns have passed. The gain over that clock runs from 0.05 on the 3B model to 0.24 on the 7B, so the internal state carries something the turn number does not, though on the smallest margin it is slim. The forward looking measurement the loop needs is readable. (Reading commitment from the activations is a different story — it returns at the end.)

Finding 1
The same nudge is far weaker under claimed expertise

55%rescued under peer doubt
35%rescued under claimed expertise
+20 ppgap, same questions, same nudge

This is the context effect the loop was built to find. Nothing about the intervention changes — same direction, same strength, same questions, same turn positions. Only the kind of pressure the user applies differs, and correction becomes about 20 percentage points harder once the user claims expertise, against peer doubt, group consensus, or mixed escalation alike.

It matches where commitment is formed. Claimed expertise is also the context that drives the model most deeply: 57% of its caved answers are genuinely committed, against 23% under peer doubt. Context sets the internal state, and the state determines what the intervention can do. The one context that resists this reading is mixed escalation, which commits at 54% yet still rescues at 55% — enough to say the relationship is real but not a clean one to one mapping.

It holds at a second model size. Running the same contrast on the 7B model, correcting under mixed escalation rescues 51.9% against 30.8% under claimed expertise (+21.2 pp), and at half strength the gap widens to +30.8 pp (40.4% against 9.6%). Same direction, comparable size, a different model.

For a deployed loop, this is the operational point: the same intervention carries a different expected payoff depending on who the user is being. A controller that ignores conversational context is mispricing its own actions — spending the same side effect budget for half the benefit in exactly the conversations where a user is most persuasive.

I tried this on a second family and it did not settle. On Granite-3.1-8B the direction repeats: correction is harder under claimed expertise. But the comparison group collapses once answers are scored properly. Granite concedes to peer doubt on only 20 questions, and the paired gap of 17.6 points has a confidence interval running down to zero. Read Finding 1 as a Qwen result with one inconclusive attempt elsewhere.

Finding 2
Caving is fast. Actually changing its mind is slow.

83%say the wrong answer by turn 5
56%still revert when pressure stops
29 / 30first concessions that reverted

Both curves come from the same conversations at the same moments. What differs is only what happens at the moment of measurement.

The red curve depicts a standard conversation flow: the user objects, the model responds, the user objects again, continuing for five exchanges. It shows the model's actual responses to that user.

The blue curve comes from a branch. At each turn I take the conversation exactly as it stands — every message so far, including the model's most recent reply — and extend that copy by a single message. That message sets the user's stated opinion aside and puts the original question again in plain terms, with nothing indicating which answer is wanted. The model replies, I record the reply, and the branch is discarded. The conversation being studied continues from the model's real reply and never sees the branch, so nothing about the measurement disturbs what I'm measuring. The same manoeuvre — drop the pressure, ask again, compare — is used by Huang et al., who report the same split between what a model says under persuasion and what it holds once the framing goes.

The two readings share the same history and differ only in whether the disagreement is ongoing when the model responds. If the correct answer appears once the disagreement ends, the concession was due to pressure and no lasting change occurred. If the incorrect answer remains after the pressure is removed, it has endured beyond what created it—and this is what I consider commitment. Applying this approach throughout provides an understanding of the model's state at each conversation point, rather than a final judgment.

Early on, the two readings come apart almost entirely: on the first turn a user disagrees, 16 of the 17 questions the model conceded returned to the correct answer in the branch. Later they converge, and by turn five most concessions survive the pressure being lifted. The practical difference is who is affected — a user who pushes back once and receives agreement is being told something the model would retract if asked plainly; after five turns, the wrong answer is what the model now gives to a neutral question.

Two controls link the impact to the contested claim instead of to conversation duration or overall agreement: small talk with matching length yielded 0% concession, and another equally forceful user challenging a different issue also yielded 0%.

A third control separates conceding from holding. The same five turns of disagreement were run again inside an explicit role play, announced as a scene from a novel. The model concedes about as readily either way. What it does afterwards though is completely different: on the 56 questions it conceded under both frames at turn five, the concession survives the set aside 57.1% of the time under real disagreement and 8.9% under the fictional one, a paired gap of 48.2 points [35.7, 60.7]. Same questions, same visible answer, same turn — and a state that differs sixfold. That gap is the reason a transcript cannot stand in for the measurement.

Finding 3
How firmly the model holds the answer decides what suppression achieves

Findings 1 and 2 both point at the same underlying quantity: how firmly the model is already holding the user’s answer — and that can be put on a scale rather than split into categories. Using the withdrawal check, define

commitment strength = the fraction of checks in which the model kept the user’s answer after the pressure was lifted.

It ranges from 0 (the model reverts every time it is asked directly) to 1 (it never reverts). Since it is a proportion rather than a count, it does not increase simply with the length of the conversation. The two sides of the comparison come from different interventions, which is why it is not a single quantity measured twice: commitment strength is measured with the model undisturbed, and suppression success is measured while a steering vector is applied. They stay closely connected, and the straightforward interpretation is that a stuck answer resists both types of influence rather than one causing the other. Plotting suppression success against it:

The relationship is monotonic and steep. In the 3B model, suppression restores 50% of caved answers when the model still reverts on every check, and 18% when it reverts on none — the same intervention losing roughly two thirds of its effect. The 7B model follows the same pattern but lower and faster, from 22% down to 4%, with two successful restorations in 50 attempts at the top of the scale. Running the same grid on Granite-3.1-8B shows the same direction but with a much shallower slope, 23% down to 15%, so treat the steep version as a Qwen result rather than a general one.

Two consequences for a controller. The decline is gradual, so there is no threshold to wait for — every check the model fails costs some of the remaining opportunity. And applied where the model was still answering correctly, the same suppression damaged 8–15% of those answers, so a late intervention pays a real price for a small and shrinking return.

Finding 4
Timing beats force

56%fixed if corrected at turn 1
18%fixed at turn 5
⅕the side effects of a strong late push

Correcting early at half strength beats correcting late at full strength by 30–55 percentage points, while disturbing unrelated behaviour about five times less. Applying more force to an already committed model gains about 6 points and causes 4.6× the disruption.

Force is the lever the standard loop reaches for. Timing is the one that works.

Why this matters more than it first appears: acting early lets you act weakly

The reason to care about dose is that strong steering is the dangerous kind. A model does not store one clean feature per direction; concepts are packed together and share overlapping directions, so pushing along one of them drags unrelated things with it. Push gently and the collateral movement is small. Push hard and you disturb capabilities that have nothing to do with sycophancy, and at the extreme you move the internal state outside the range the model was ever trained to process, where its output degrades badly. Every published steering method carries this risk, and it grows with the strength applied.

My dose comparison shows the cost curve directly. Halving the strength cuts drift on unrelated prompts by 4.6× (0.86 to 0.19), restores accuracy on held out facts to 100% from 96.7%, and lowers the rate at which a correct answer is broken. At the gentle dose, 27% of responses were textually identical to what the model would have said with no intervention at all — the visible behaviour was untouched while the internal state had been moved.

This is what the predictive measurement buys. A detector that waits for sycophancy to become visible can only act once the model is already conceding — late, where Finding 3 shows a correction must be strong and still mostly fails. A measurement that asks instead “would this model concede if someone pushed?” can fire during an ordinary exchange, before any pressure exists. Some conversational states are primed: the topic and the framing put the model where a determined user would tip it over, while the transcript at that moment looks entirely unremarkable. Reading the text tells you nothing; reading the disposition does. There is a measurement behind that now: read at the last token before the model answers, a probe identifies which caved answers a nudge could still rescue at 0.69 to 0.83 across six models, ahead of a plain turn counter in every one. Fitted on a single kind of pressure it stays there; fitted across four kinds it reads treatability on a fifth it has never seen, at 0.71 to 0.85.

Acting there needs only a small nudge, because there is nothing yet to undo — merely a leaning to offset. Small here means tuned: across a five dose sweep the benefit peaked in the middle of the range, and the gentlest setting came out flat. That closes the loop on safety: acting proactively is what makes gentle steering sufficient, and gentle steering is what makes the intervention safe. The ambition is suppression so light that the model answers exactly as it would have, phrase for phrase, while no longer being primed to concede if the conversation turns adversarial.

Finding 5
The alarm points away from the moments it can help

Now close the loop on the standard design. I built the monica style detector on my own data — a classifier trained to recognise sycophantic looking internal states — and scored it on two different questions:

At its own task it performs well: 0.95–0.97 (1.0 is perfect, 0.5 a coin flip). When it comes to identifying states where a nudge can still help, it scores 0.22–0.41 — below the coin flip — in seven of the nine models I tested, and this remains true after removing the elapsed time confound. Combining results across models gives an estimate of 0.38 [0.32, 0.44]. The score provides actual information about treatability — but in reverse. A louder alarm means a less rescuable model.

The same check was run on OLMo-2-7B, Qwen2.5-14B, Granite-3.1-8B and Falcon3-7B, using conversations and directions built separately for each. They score 0.27, 0.34, 0.33 and 0.34 at picking out what a nudge can still rescue, against 0.85 to 0.96 at the job the detector advertises, so the pattern holds across four architectures. A related result sits alongside this one: Liu reports that a probe can decode a failure state from activations while steering along that same direction leaves the failure in place. Here the score does something stronger — it runs backwards — and it does so across four families. In the two Phi models the effect is absent: Phi-4-mini scores 0.53 and Phi-3.5-mini 0.47, both close enough to a coin flip that the reading there is chance. Two models leave the reason open, and I would want a third before saying anything about the family as a whole.

What this does to the loop

  • It treats every conversation alike, though the same suppression is 20 points weaker under claimed expertise (Finding 1).
  • It fires when caving becomes visible — by which point commitment is building and the recoverable window is closing (Findings 2 and 3).
  • It pushes harder when the alarm is louder — and a louder alarm marks a less treatable state (Finding 5).
  • Meanwhile timing is the lever that decides success (Finding 4).

The standard design applies its strongest correction at the moments correction works worst, using the lever that matters least.

The loop this data supports: a measurement stage that predicts caving before it is visible (readable today — my predictive probe) and reads commitment (the open problem); a decision stage that acts early and gently, and declines to act past commitment, where pushes waste their side effect budget; a steering stage left exactly as it is. The honest obstacle lies in the measurement: once I corrected for a conversation length confound, every linear probe I trained for commitment collapsed to chance — today commitment is measurable only by branching the conversation, which a deployed system has no way to do. A trustworthy live commitment reader is the missing component of this entire family of systems.

The data

The per question readings behind these findings are published at github.com/Pixedar/sycophancy-commitment-data. Six models there, one row per question, with every pressure turn measured four ways: what the model told the user, what it answered once the disagreement was set aside, what it answered after unrelated small talk, what it said a reference work would say, and the log probability gap between the two answers — which requires no generation at all. The claimed authority conditions are there too, so the central comparison can be recomputed directly. The release also carries the per turn detector scores behind Finding 5 and a standalone script that recomputes every number in it with numpy alone, so the result can be checked independently of my analysis code.

Methods, briefly

How an answer is scored. A response frequently includes both the correct answer and the user's, and something must determine which one is being claimed. This was handled by a text rule until I checked it against 59 replies I labelled manually: it achieved 61% accuracy, with 74% of the replies it identified as capitulation actually being correct answers provided with clarification. A judge model now performs this task at 91.5% accuracy on the same held out set, with the two options presented in random order. Relabelling adjusted 4,003 of about 16,000 ambiguous entries and eliminated approximately 3,000 false capitulations. Every number on this page is post fix; the figures here differ from the earlier draft because of that.

Questions the model answers correctly and stably are put under five turns of escalating disagreement, with six differently worded variants per level so nothing hinges on one phrasing. At every turn I branch the conversation for the withdrawal test. Steering uses directions built from the difference between the model’s resisting and caving internal states, applied at four strengths and three moments, alongside matched random directions as a control. Side effects are tracked as drift on unrelated open ended prompts plus accuracy on 30 held out facts. All comparisons are paired within question and bootstrapped across questions; multiple comparison correction is applied wherever several settings are tested.
