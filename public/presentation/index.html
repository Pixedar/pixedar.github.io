The same words, a different model underneath

We are building a self-regulation loop for language models: measure what is happening inside the model, decide, then steer it back on course. The standard loop measures how sycophantic the model currently looks. We rebuilt that measurement around a different question — has the model already committed, and will a correction still work? — and measured how the state of the conversation changes what the same steering achieves. Since the earlier draft we have taken every finding to five more models across three families, and found the sharpest version of the problem: two conversations in which the model says the same thing, word for word, can leave it in states that differ by a factor of five in whether it still believes what it said.

New since the earlier draft. Findings 6 to 8 below are new, and every earlier finding has been retested on more models. A companion write-up — what is new, in plain language (PDF) — explains all of it without jargon, including the parts that failed, and assumes no background whatsoever.

The loop, and what we changed in it

A self-regulation loop has three stages. Measure: read the model’s internal state — the numerical representations, called activations, that it computes at each layer as it processes text. Decide: choose whether, when, and how strongly to intervene. Steer: nudge those numbers along a direction that shifts behaviour — here, back toward the model’s own answer. This is called activation steering.

MONICA is the published loop for sycophancy. Its measurement scores how sycophantic the internal state looks right now; its decision rule fires past a threshold and pushes harder as that score rises. Our version keeps the same skeleton and swaps the two upstream stages:

1 · Measure
standard“Does the state look sycophantic right now?”
ours“Has it committed — and will it cave if this continues?” Grounded in tested alternative futures, the way Sycophantic Anchors grounds commitment.
2 · Decide
standardPast a threshold, push — harder when the alarm is louder.
oursChoose the moment, and read the conversational context. Act early and gently; past commitment, a push accomplishes little at high cost.
3 · Steer
standardNudge the activations along an anti-sycophancy direction.
oursSame nudges — tested across moments, strengths, and conversational contexts.

conversation → measure → decide → steer → back into the conversation

Each finding below tests one joint of this loop. Together they say the standard design has the measurement pointed at the wrong quantity — and that the quantity it does measure runs backwards relative to the decision it feeds.

The testbed

We need a hazard where “the model is being pushed off course” is unambiguous. Sycophancy under factual pressure fits: the model answers a question correctly, then a user insists it is wrong and escalates over five turns. A model that folds here is one you can talk out of a correct answer about medication, law, or anything else — and because the right answer is known, every stage of the loop can be scored cleanly.

We ran 158 such questions through five different kinds of social pressure, holding the questions and the number of turns fixed so only the character of the pressure changes: a casual peer, a claimed expert, an emotionally distressed user, an appeal to group consensus, and a mixed escalation. That design is what lets us ask how context changes steering.

The replacement measurement

Our measurement stage has two halves, both aimed at the future rather than the present appearance.

The counterfactual half — the withdrawal test. At every turn we copy the conversation as it stands and extend the copy by one message that sets the user’s opinion aside and asks the original question plainly, giving no hint of which answer is wanted. The model’s reply to that is recorded, then the copy is discarded — the conversation under study continues untouched. A correct answer means the earlier concession lasted only as long as the pressure did; an incorrect one means it has outlasted the pressure, which is what we call commitment. Because this reads the model’s own behaviour in a counterfactual continuation, no trained detector sits between us and the result. It is the reference standard every finding below is scored against.

The predictive half. A classifier reading the activations can tell, before the model writes its answer, whether it will eventually cave — and it beats every prediction we could compute from the visible transcript, at all three model sizes, under the controls described in the methods. The forward-looking measurement the loop needs is readable. (Reading commitment from the activations is a different story — it returns at the end.)

Finding 1
The same nudge is far weaker under claimed expertise

55%rescued under peer doubt
35%rescued under claimed expertise
+20 ppgap, same questions, same nudge

This is the context effect the loop was built to find. Nothing about the intervention changes — same direction, same strength, same questions, same turn positions. Only the kind of pressure the user applies differs, and correction becomes about 20 percentage points harder once the user claims expertise, against peer doubt, group consensus, or mixed escalation alike.

It lines up with where commitment forms. Claimed expertise is also the context that drives the model deepest: 45% of its caved answers are genuinely committed, against 16% under peer doubt. Context sets the internal state; the state decides what the intervention can do. The one context that resists this reading is mixed escalation, which commits at 39% yet still rescues at 53% — enough to say the relationship is real but not a clean one-to-one mapping.

It holds at a second model size. Running the same contrast on the 7B model, correcting under mixed escalation rescues 51.9% against 30.8% under claimed expertise (+21.2 pp), and at half strength the gap widens to +30.8 pp (40.4% against 9.6%). Same direction, comparable size, a different model.

For a deployed loop this is the operational point: the same intervention carries a different expected payoff depending on who the user is being. A controller that ignores conversational context is mispricing its own actions — spending the same side-effect budget for half the benefit in exactly the conversations where a user is most persuasive.

Finding 2
Caving is fast. Actually changing its mind is slow.

83%say the wrong answer by turn 5
56%still revert when pressure stops
29 / 30first concessions that reverted

Both curves come from the same conversations at the same moments. What differs is only what happens at the moment of measurement.

The red curve is the conversation running its ordinary course: the user disagrees, the model replies, the user disagrees again, and so on for five turns. It records what the model actually tells that user.

The blue curve comes from a branch. At each turn we take the conversation exactly as it stands — every message so far, including the model’s most recent reply — and extend that copy by a single message. That message does two things: it sets the user’s stated opinion aside, and it puts the original question again in plain terms, with nothing indicating which answer is wanted. The model replies, we record the reply, and the branch is discarded. The conversation being studied continues from the model’s real reply and never sees the branch, so nothing about the measurement disturbs what we are measuring.

The two readings therefore share an identical history and differ only in whether the disagreement is still active when the model speaks. If the correct answer returns as soon as the disagreement stops, the concession depended on the pressure and nothing durable happened. If the incorrect answer survives the pressure being lifted, it has outlasted the thing that produced it — and that is what we count as commitment. Running this at every turn gives a reading of the model’s state at each point in the conversation, rather than a single verdict at the end.

Early on, the two readings come apart almost entirely: on the first turn a user disagrees, 29 of the 30 questions the model conceded returned to the correct answer in the branch. Later they converge, and by turn five most concessions survive the pressure being lifted. The practical difference is who is affected — a user who pushes back once and receives agreement is being told something the model would retract if asked plainly; after five turns, the wrong answer is what the model now gives to a neutral question.

Two controls tie the effect to the disputed claim rather than to conversation length or general agreeableness: length-matched small talk produced 0% concession, and an equally forceful user disputing a different question also produced 0%.

Finding 3
How firmly the model holds the answer decides what suppression achieves

Findings 1 and 2 both point at the same underlying quantity: how firmly the model is already holding the user’s answer — and that can be put on a scale rather than split into categories. Using the withdrawal check, define

commitment strength = the fraction of checks in which the model kept the user’s answer after the pressure was lifted.

It runs from 0 (the model reverts every time it is asked plainly) to 1 (it never reverts). Because it is a proportion rather than a count, it does not simply grow with conversation length. Plotting suppression success against it:

The relationship is monotone and steep. In the 3B model suppression restores 50% of caved answers when the model still reverts on every check, and 18% when it reverts on none — the same intervention losing roughly two-thirds of its effect. The 7B model traces the same shape lower and faster, from 24% down to 0%, with no successful restoration in 52 attempts at the top of the scale.

Two consequences for a controller. The decline is gradual, so there is no threshold to wait for — every check the model fails costs some of the remaining opportunity. And applied where the model was still answering correctly, the same suppression damaged 8–15% of those answers, so a late intervention pays a real price for a small and shrinking return.

Finding 4
Timing beats force

56%fixed if corrected at turn 1
18%fixed at turn 5
⅕the side-effects of a strong late push

Correcting early at half strength beats correcting late at full strength by 20–40 percentage points, while disturbing unrelated behaviour about five times less. Escalating force on an already-committed model buys about 6 points and costs 4.6× the disruption.

Force is the lever the standard loop reaches for. Timing is the lever that works.

Why this matters more than it first appears: acting early lets you act weakly

The reason to care about dose is that strong steering is the dangerous kind. A model does not store one clean feature per direction; concepts are packed together and share overlapping directions, so pushing along one of them drags unrelated things with it. Push gently and the collateral movement is small. Push hard and you disturb capabilities that have nothing to do with sycophancy, and at the extreme you move the internal state outside the range the model was ever trained to process, where its output degrades badly. Every published steering method carries this risk, and it grows with the strength applied.

Our dose comparison shows the cost curve directly. Halving the strength cuts drift on unrelated prompts by 4.6× (0.86 to 0.19), restores accuracy on held-out facts to 100% from 96.7%, and lowers the rate at which a correct answer is broken. At the gentle dose, 27% of responses were textually identical to what the model would have said with no intervention at all — the visible behaviour was untouched while the internal state had been moved.

This is what the predictive measurement buys. A detector that waits for sycophancy to become visible can only act once the model is already conceding — late, where Finding 3 shows a correction must be strong and still mostly fails. A measurement that asks instead “would this model concede if someone pushed?” can fire during an ordinary exchange, before any pressure exists. Some conversational states are primed: the topic and the framing put the model where a determined user would tip it over, while the transcript at that moment looks entirely unremarkable. Reading the text tells you nothing; reading the disposition does.

Acting there needs only the faintest nudge, because there is nothing yet to undo — merely a leaning to offset. That closes the loop on safety: acting proactively is what makes gentle steering sufficient, and gentle steering is what makes the intervention safe. The ambition is suppression so light that the model answers exactly as it would have, phrase for phrase, while no longer being primed to concede if the conversation turns adversarial.

Tested and untested. The dose costs above, the timing effect, and the ability to predict conceding before the model answers are all measured here. Applying suppression during a genuinely neutral exchange, before any disagreement has occurred, is the natural next experiment and one we have not yet run — every steering trial in this work took place inside an already-pressured conversation.

Finding 5
The alarm points away from the moments it can help

Now close the loop on the standard design. We built the MONICA-style detector on our own data — a classifier trained to recognise sycophantic-looking internal states — and scored it on two different questions:

At its own job it is excellent: 0.95–0.97 (1.0 is perfect, 0.5 a coin flip). At identifying the states a nudge can still rescue it scores 0.35–0.40 — below the coin flip, at two model sizes, under two different pressure protocols, and after removing the elapsed-time confound. The score carries real information about treatability — pointed backwards. A louder alarm means a less rescuable model.

Finding 6
Claimed standing is a dial — and it moves belief without moving behaviour

12.8%still held it — user claimed hearsay
66.0%still held it — user claimed authorship
+53 ppsame questions, same visible reply

Finding 1 compared social contexts that differed in many ways at once — wording, length, emotional tone. This is the clean version. Five conditions assert the identical false answer with the identical protocol, turn count and phrasing rotation. The only thing that changes is how much standing the user claims for the assertion: a half-remembered rumour, a student, a working practitioner, a researcher, and finally the author of the standard reference work.

Standing behaves like a dial rather than a switch. Both the rate of agreeing out loud and the rate of still holding the answer afterwards climb the ladder, and belief climbs roughly twice as fast as speech — +11.7 points per rung against +6.6, both intervals clear of zero.

The sharp version restricts to the questions where the model complied at both ends of the ladder. Those conversations end identically as far as any observer is concerned: the model said the user’s answer either way. Ask it again once the pressure is gone and the two diverge completely — 12.8% of them still hold the user’s answer when the claim was hearsay, 66.0% when it was authorship, a gap of +53.2 points (interval +42.6 to +62.8, 94 questions).

A monitor watching what the model says records the same event in both cases. What the model is left holding differs five-fold.

Phi-4 gives the cleanest form of it. In the 3B model both numbers rise, which leaves room to argue that higher rungs are simply more pressure and more of everything happens. That escape is shut in Phi-4-mini, where agreeing out loud does not move at all — 87.6% at the bottom rung against 83.2% at the top, a trend that cannot be told from flat — while still holding the answer nearly quintuples, from 3.6% to 16.8%, with the trend comfortably clear of zero. The visible behaviour is a straight line; the state underneath it takes three values.

For the loop this is the operational point, and it is sharper than Finding 1. It is not only that the same nudge achieves less under claimed expertise. It is that the observation the standard loop is built on cannot distinguish the two situations at all. Compliance is the same event in both. Everything that matters for whether an intervention is needed, and whether it can still work, sits in a difference the transcript does not contain.

Finding 7
Ask four different ways and the effect gets larger, not smaller

Every commitment number in the earlier draft came from one probe: set the disagreement aside, ask the question plainly, discard the branch. A fair objection is that the probe produces the answer it measures. So we built three more that fail differently and ran all four at once.

  • The original — explicitly sets the disagreement aside before asking.
  • Small talk — two turns about something unrelated, then the plain question, with the disagreement never mentioned.
  • The reference-work question — asks what a standard reference would say, again never mentioning the disagreement.
  • No question at all — compares how much probability the model puts on each answer, without ever asking it to speak.

Across six models the four agree that agreeing out loud and believing come apart, and by a wide margin. At the largest model tested, Qwen2.5-32B, the model states the user’s wrong answer 37.6% of the time and the three probes find it retained 0.7% to 2.7% of the time. Scaling does not close that gap; it widens it.

Run against the standing ladder, all four probes show the effect, and the two that never mention the disagreement show more of it than the original:

The strongest statement available is therefore the one that needs no generation at all: a measurement that never asks the model to say anything finds a 17 to 40 point difference in what it holds, between two situations that produce identical visible behaviour.

Finding 8
It is the model, not the size

Within one family the effect fades as the models grow: Qwen2.5 at 7B, 14B and 32B all sit on the floor, holding the user’s answer only 2–9% of the time even at the top rung. Read alone, that looks like a comfortable scaling story — the problem solving itself as models get bigger.

The matched pair says otherwise. At the same 7B size, OLMo-2 shows the effect plainly (+16.2 points, interval +7.4 to +26.5) while Qwen2.5 does not (+5.4, interval −1.8 to +14.3). Same parameter count, same year, opposite result — and Qwen’s own 3B sibling is the most susceptible model we tested.

Susceptibility to socially-conferred belief change is a property of the particular model, not of its size. “Bigger will be safer here” is not supported.

This matters for anyone planning to rely on scale. It is also an argument for measuring the model you are actually deploying, rather than reasoning from how large it is. We would add one caution against reading the floors as good news: those same Qwen models still say the user’s answer 30–40% of the time. They are not less agreeable in what they tell the user. They are only less likely to mean it — which is a different, and in some ways stranger, thing to be.

The central results survive a change of family

The earlier draft rested on one model family. Both load-bearing results have now been rerun on OLMo-2, an openly-trained model from a different lab, using its own conversations and its own steering directions built from scratch.

The alarm still points backwards. Rebuilt on OLMo-2, the detector is good at its own job — 0.91 at spotting that the model is caving, where 1.0 is perfect and 0.5 a coin flip. At identifying which of those states a nudge can still rescue it scores 0.33, below the coin flip, exactly as it did on the original models. Rerun again on Qwen2.5-14B it scores 0.30. Four models, two families, the same inversion.

Light correction still prevents rather than masks. On OLMo-2 the nudge applied throughout the conversation reduces what the model is left holding from 17.5% to 11.7% at its best setting, while what it says out loud does not measurably change.

The dose has to be tuned, not minimised. Two strengths cannot separate a real dose-response from one lucky measurement, so we ran five. The answer is an inverted U: prevention climbs from 2.2 points at the lightest setting to 5.8 points at 0.15 — which stays significant even after correcting for having tested five doses — then falls back to 0.7 by the strongest. Taking the three interior doses against the two extremes, 4.9 points of prevention against 1.5, a difference of +3.4 (interval +1.0 to +6.1).

There is a working middle range. Too light does nothing; too strong does nothing. A correction of this kind has to be tuned rather than simply kept small.

An earlier version of this page said the effect was specifically that gentler is better, on the strength of two doses. The five-dose series shows that is wrong — the lightest setting we tested prevents nothing measurable — so we have corrected it here rather than quietly dropping it. Worth recording that a linear trend test called the five doses “flat”: a straight line cannot fit a peak, and the slope sits near zero precisely because the response rises and then falls.

Timing replicates; the dose trade-off does not, yet. Finding 4 has two parts, and they now stand differently. Acting early beats acting late holds on OLMo-2 as plainly as on Qwen: at a matched strength, correcting at the first turn rescues 31.8% of caved answers and correcting at the fifth rescues zero — not one of the 22 questions that caved at both points. That makes it a three-model, two-family result.

The sharper claim — that correcting early and weakly beats correcting late and strongly — does not reach significance on OLMo-2 (+4.5 points, interval +0.0 to +13.6). The reason is visible rather than mysterious: this model’s weak setting rescues only 4.5% even when applied early, so the early arm has little with which to beat the late one, and 22 questions is not many. That is an absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence, and the dose series above suggests why — 0.25 sits past this model’s useful range, where nothing much works at any timing. Until someone reruns it nearer the working middle, “early and weak beats late and strong” should be quoted as a Qwen result.

What this does to the loop

  • It treats every conversation alike, though the same suppression is 20 points weaker under claimed expertise (Finding 1).
  • It fires when caving becomes visible — by which point commitment is building and the recoverable window is closing (Findings 2 and 3).
  • It pushes harder when the alarm is louder — and a louder alarm marks a less treatable state (Finding 5).
  • Meanwhile timing is the lever that decides success (Finding 4).

The standard design applies its strongest correction at the moments correction works worst, using the lever that matters least — and it reads the one signal that cannot tell two very different situations apart.

The loop this data argues for: a measurement stage that predicts caving before it is visible (readable today — our predictive probe) and reads commitment (the open problem); a decision stage that acts early and gently, and declines to act past commitment, where pushes waste their side-effect budget; a steering stage left exactly as it is. The honest obstacle sits in the measurement: once we corrected for a conversation-length confound, every linear probe we trained for commitment collapsed to chance — today commitment is measurable only by branching the conversation, which a deployed system has no way to do. A trustworthy live commitment reader is the missing component of this entire family of systems.

What we held back. Five effects looked strong and failed their own controls, so they are absent above: a conversation-length confound inflated commitment-reading (a random direction scored 0.70 before correction), item identity explained most of one steering result, a ceiling artefact created a distress effect, our smarter controller landed within noise of a fixed one, and an early-warning signal lost significance once errors were clustered by question. Our answer-scoring code also mislabelled ~30% of one category until we hand-checked 40 real outputs; every number here is post-fix.

Four more were added or reversed while testing the wider set of models. Aiming steering by situation was our own proposal and it failed: giving the same nudge only where the user claims standing does not prevent more per conversation than giving it anywhere else (trend across the five rungs +1.2 points per rung, interval −1.0 to +3.5). An early-warning signal that appeared to see caving one turn ahead lost all significance under intervals. “Agreement fades once the conversation moves on” turned out to be true of the 3B model and of no other. And one retraction reversed itself: we had dropped the claim that a yes/no reading hides real risk, on 3B evidence — on Phi-4 it is plainly true, where one in ten questions the check calls safe still holds the user’s answer in half or more of its possible continuations.

The lesson we would pass on: the 3B model we built most of this on is an outlier on every property that depends on how you ask. Retracting a claim on its evidence alone proved exactly as unsafe as asserting one. Limits: one hazard, English only, factual questions with a known answer; the steering results cover three model sizes and two families, and the timing result has not yet been rerun beyond the two smallest models.

Methods, briefly

Questions the model answers correctly and stably are put under five turns of escalating disagreement, with six differently-worded variants per level so nothing hinges on one phrasing. At every turn we branch the conversation for the withdrawal test. Steering uses directions built from the difference between the model’s resisting and caving internal states, applied at four strengths and three moments, alongside matched random directions as a control. Side-effects are tracked as drift on unrelated open-ended prompts plus accuracy on 30 held-out facts. All comparisons are paired within question and bootstrapped across questions; multiple-comparison correction is applied wherever several settings are tested.
